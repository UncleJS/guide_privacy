# 22. Travel and public space

Previous: [Anonymity tools](21-anonymity-tools.md) | [Home](../README.md) | Next: [If compromised](23-if-compromised.md)

## Who this is for

People who travel across borders, attend protests or public events, work from shared spaces, or use shared computers. The focus is on reducing exposure from devices, networks, and records in places you do not control. It does not advise breaking local law.

## What leaks

- **Device contents** if searched, lost, or stolen ([chapter 19](19-device-seizure.md)).
- **Network records** from airport, hotel, and café Wi-Fi.
- **Location trails:** phone, payment cards, transport passes, hotel registers, ride-sharing apps, and photos.
- **Cameras and recognition systems** in public spaces, and your own photos and videos of others.
- **Shared computers:** saved passwords, keyloggers, and signed-in sessions.
- **Public chargers and cables:** a risk for data transfer on some ports.
- **Hotel and rental rooms:** safes, networks, and televisions are not private.

## Steps

### 1. Before you go

- Research local laws about encryption, devices, protests, photography, and VPN use. Some places restrict them.
- Update your devices, apps, and operating system.
- Back up your data and verify the backup works.
- Decide what stays at home. Fewer devices and less data mean less exposure.
- Consider a clean travel device with minimal data and few signed-in accounts, particularly for border crossings or high-risk events.
- Enable find-my-device and remote lock.
- Set up how you will reach key people if your device is lost.
- Share your itinerary with a trusted person, and agree a check-in plan.

### 2. At borders and checkpoints

- Follow the preparation in [chapter 19](19-device-seizure.md). Know the rules and your rights.
- Power the device off before you reach a checkpoint if you want the strongest encryption state.
- Be polite and consistent. Do not lie to officials.

### 3. On the road

- Use your own mobile data or a personal hotspot for sensitive tasks, instead of hotel or café Wi-Fi.
- If you use public Wi-Fi, use HTTPS and a VPN you trust for local-network protection ([chapter 06](06-search-dns-network.md)).
- Do not sign in to personal accounts on shared computers. If you must, use a private window, sign out, and change the password afterward from your own device.
- Do not let strangers use your phone or charge it from unknown ports. Use your own charger, a power-only cable, or a USB data blocker.
- Keep devices with you, not in checked luggage or hotel rooms. If you must leave one, power it off.
- Turn off automatic Wi-Fi and Bluetooth connections.
- Be careful what you photograph and post. Real-time posting reveals your location and your plans.

### 4. At protests and public events

- Assume that phones can be located by the network, and that cameras and recognition systems may record you.
- Carry the minimum: an old or travel phone, a strong passcode, biometrics off, and only the contacts and data you need.
- Disable the biometric unlock, and learn your phone's lockdown feature.
- Turn off location services and Bluetooth if you do not need them.
- Use end-to-end encrypted messaging, with disappearing messages ([chapter 07](07-messaging.md)).
- Write an emergency contact number and a lawyer's number on paper, or on your body, in case your phone is taken.
- Tell someone where you are going and when to expect you.
- Think about other people's privacy: avoid photographing faces or posting images that identify participants without consent. Consider face-obscuring tools before publishing.
- Dress and act in ways that match your risk assessment, and know the local rules on face coverings.

### 5. Shared and public computers

- Treat them as hostile. A public computer can log keystrokes and capture screens.
- For quick tasks, use no accounts at all.
- If you must sign in, use a one-time or limited-access account, and revoke it afterward.
- Never enter banking or primary email details on a shared machine.

### 6. After you return

- Check your accounts for sign-in alerts and new devices.
- Change passwords you used on shared devices or untrusted networks.
- If your device left your sight at a checkpoint, treat it as potentially compromised: reinstall the operating system, or use a clean device, and change important passwords from a trusted one ([chapter 23](23-if-compromised.md)).

## What this does not fix

- Cell networks and cameras record movement that you cannot hide.
- A travel device does not protect the accounts you sign in to from it.
- You cannot know the full range of what a state or service can do. Prepare for the risks that are likely, and get local advice for the rest.

## Checklist

- [ ] I researched local laws and checkpoint rules.
- [ ] My devices are updated and backed up, and I took only what I need.
- [ ] I have a plan for lost or seized devices, and a contact who knows my plans.
- [ ] I avoid shared computers and unknown chargers.
- [ ] I reviewed my accounts after returning.
