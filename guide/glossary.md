## 0-9

**3-2-1 backup rule.** Keep three copies of your data, on two kinds of storage, with one off-site. See [chapter 11](11-files-cloud-backups.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## A

**Account takeover.** An attacker gaining control of one of your accounts, usually through a reused password, phishing, or a SIM swap. See [chapter 16](16-account-takeover.md).

**ACR (automatic content recognition).** A smart-TV feature that identifies what is on screen and reports viewing data to the manufacturer or advertisers. See [chapter 13](13-home-and-devices.md).

**Advertising ID.** A device-level identifier that lets ad networks follow you across apps. See [chapter 09](09-phones.md).

**AES / XTS-AES.** AES is a widely used encryption standard. XTS-AES is the mode commonly used for disk encryption. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Alias.** A secondary email address that forwards to your main inbox, used to avoid sharing your real address. See [chapter 04](04-email.md).

**Anonymity.** The inability of others to link an action to your real identity. Distinct from privacy and security. See [chapter 01](01-privacy-security-anonymity.md).

**Attack surface.** All the ways an attacker could try to get in: installed software, open services, ports, extensions, and accounts. Fewer means safer. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Authenticator app.** An app that generates time-based one-time codes for two-factor authentication. See [chapter 03](03-accounts.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## B

**Biometrics.** Fingerprint, face, or similar unlock methods. Convenient, but in some places easier for authorities to compel than a passcode. See [chapter 09](09-phones.md) and [chapter 19](19-device-seizure.md).

**BitLocker.** Windows full-disk encryption. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Bridge (Tor).** An unlisted entry point to the Tor network, used where Tor is blocked or where being seen to use Tor is risky. See [chapter 21](21-anonymity-tools.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## C

**Captive portal.** The sign-in or terms page shown by public Wi-Fi before you can connect. Do not enter passwords on one you do not recognize. See [chapter 06](06-search-dns-network.md).

**CCPA / CPRA.** California's consumer-privacy laws, which give residents rights to access, delete, and opt out of the sale of personal data. See [chapter 15](15-data-brokers-and-breaches.md).

**Cold-boot attack.** Recovering encryption keys from a computer's memory shortly after it is shut down or while it is asleep. Shutting down fully and waiting reduces the risk. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Compartmentalization.** Keeping identities, accounts, and devices separate so that a problem in one does not expose the others. See [chapter 18](18-compartments.md).

**Content blocker.** A browser extension that blocks ads and many trackers, such as uBlock Origin. See [chapter 05](05-browsers.md).

**Cookie (third-party).** A small file a website stores in your browser. Third-party cookies come from other companies embedded in the page and let them recognize you across sites. See [chapter 05](05-browsers.md).

**Credential stuffing.** An attack that tries leaked email and password pairs on other sites, relying on reuse.

**Credit freeze.** A lock on your credit file that blocks new credit in your name until you lift it. See [chapter 15](15-data-brokers-and-breaches.md).

**Cryptographic erase.** Making encrypted data unreadable by destroying the encryption key instead of overwriting the data. Fast and reliable for retiring encrypted drives. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## D

**Data breach.** An incident in which a company loses or exposes data it held about you. See [chapter 15](15-data-brokers-and-breaches.md).

**Data broker.** A company that collects, combines, and sells personal data. See [chapter 15](15-data-brokers-and-breaches.md).

**Disappearing messages.** A messaging feature that deletes messages from the devices after a set time. It cannot stop a recipient from copying or screenshotting. See [chapter 07](07-messaging.md).

**DMA (direct memory access) attack.** Using a device plugged into a port such as Thunderbolt to read a computer's memory directly. DMA protection and IOMMU settings reduce the risk. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**DNS (Domain Name System).** The system that turns site names into addresses. A DNS resolver sees which sites you look up. See [chapter 06](06-search-dns-network.md).

**DNS over HTTPS / TLS.** Encrypted DNS lookups that hide queries from the local network.

**Doxxing.** Publishing someone's private information, such as their home address or workplace, to expose or harass them. See [chapter 08](08-social-media.md) and [chapter 17](17-targeted-threats.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## E

**EFI system partition.** A small partition that holds boot software. It is usually not encrypted, which is why boot protections matter. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**End-to-end encryption (E2EE).** Encryption where only the sender and recipient devices hold the keys, so the service provider cannot read the content. See [chapter 07](07-messaging.md).

**Evil maid attack.** Tampering with a computer's boot process or hardware by someone who gets brief physical access, to capture your passphrase later. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Exit relay.** The last Tor node, which connects to the destination and can see unencrypted traffic.

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## F

**FIDO2 / WebAuthn.** Standards behind hardware security keys and passkeys that resist phishing.

**FileVault.** macOS full-disk encryption. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Fingerprinting.** Identifying a browser or device from its combination of settings and features, without cookies. See [chapter 05](05-browsers.md).

**Firmware (UEFI/BIOS).** The low-level software that starts a computer before the operating system loads. A firmware password and Secure Boot protect it.

**Full-disk encryption.** Encryption of the whole storage device, protecting data when the device is off. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## G

**GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation).** The European Union's data-protection law, which gives people rights to access, correct, delete, and object to the use of their data. See [chapter 15](15-data-brokers-and-breaches.md).

**GPS (Global Positioning System).** Satellite-based positioning used by phones and other devices to find precise location. See [chapter 09](09-phones.md).

**Guard relay.** The first relay a Tor connection passes through. It sees your real IP address but not your destination. See [chapter 21](21-anonymity-tools.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## H

**Hardening.** Reducing a system's attack surface and tightening its settings so that it is harder to compromise. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Hardware security key.** A small physical device used for sign-in that resists phishing, using FIDO2/WebAuthn. See [chapter 03](03-accounts.md).

**HTTPS.** Encrypted web connections, which protect page content in transit.

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## I

**IOMMU.** A hardware feature that limits which memory a connected device can access, used for DMA protection. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**IoT (Internet of Things).** Everyday devices connected to the internet, such as cameras, speakers, TVs, and appliances. See [chapter 13](13-home-and-devices.md).

**IP address.** The network address that identifies your connection to the sites and services you use. It reveals approximate location and your internet provider. See [chapter 06](06-search-dns-network.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## K

**Keylogger.** Software or hardware that records what you type, including passwords and passphrases. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## L

**Linkage.** Connecting separate accounts, identities, or records to the same person, for example through a shared username, photo, or recovery address. See [chapter 08](08-social-media.md) and [chapter 18](18-compartments.md).

**Lockdown feature.** A phone setting or button combination that quickly disables biometrics and requires the passcode, or enables a high-security mode. See [chapter 19](19-device-seizure.md).

**LUKS.** The standard Linux disk-encryption format. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## M

**Malware.** Malicious software, including spyware, keyloggers, ransomware, and remote-access tools. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Measured boot.** A startup process in which the TPM records the boot software and only releases the disk key if it is unchanged. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Metadata.** Data about communications and activity (who, when, where, how much), as opposed to the content. See [chapter 20](20-metadata.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## O

**Onion service.** A site reachable only through Tor (an address ending in .onion), protecting both the visitor and the site. See [chapter 21](21-anonymity-tools.md).

**Open source.** Software whose code is public, so independent experts can review it. This helps, but does not guarantee that it is secure.

**OpenPGP.** A standard for encrypting and signing email and files. Powerful, but hard to use safely. See [chapter 04](04-email.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## P

**Passkey.** A cryptographic sign-in credential, stored on a device or in a password manager, that replaces a password and resists phishing. See [chapter 03](03-accounts.md).

**Passphrase.** A long password made of several random words or a long sentence. See [chapter 03](03-accounts.md).

**Password manager.** An app that generates, stores, and fills in unique passwords for every account. See [chapter 03](03-accounts.md).

**People-search site.** A website that publishes names, addresses, phone numbers, and relatives gathered from public records and data brokers. See [chapter 15](15-data-brokers-and-breaches.md).

**Phishing.** Deceptive messages or sites designed to steal credentials or money.

**Port-out protection.** A carrier setting that blocks moving your number to another provider without extra verification, used against SIM swapping.

**Privacy.** Control over who can see or collect information about you, and how it is used.

**Private browsing.** A browser mode that does not save history or cookies locally after the window closes. It does not hide your activity from websites, your network, or your internet provider. See [chapter 05](05-browsers.md).

**Pseudonym.** A name other than your legal name used for an account or identity. It reduces linkage but does not provide anonymity. See [chapter 08](08-social-media.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## Q

**Qubes OS.** A security-focused operating system that isolates activities in separate virtual machines. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## R

**Ransomware.** Malware that encrypts your files and demands payment to unlock them. Offline backups are the best defense. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**RCS (Rich Communication Services).** The newer standard that extends SMS with features such as read receipts and group chat. Not end-to-end encrypted in every case. See [chapter 07](07-messaging.md).

**RDP (Remote Desktop Protocol).** Windows remote-control feature. Leave it off unless you need it. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Recovery key.** A secret code that restores access to an encrypted account or disk. Losing it can mean permanent loss of data.

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## S

**Safety number.** A code that two people compare to confirm they are talking to each other and not to an impersonator. See [chapter 07](07-messaging.md).

**Sandbox.** An isolated environment that limits what a program can access, used by browsers, phones, and some desktop apps. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Sealed sender.** A messaging feature that hides the sender's identity from the service when delivering a message. See [chapter 20](20-metadata.md).

**Secure Boot.** A firmware feature that only starts operating-system software signed by trusted keys, making boot-time tampering harder. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Security.** Protection against unauthorized access to your accounts, devices, and data.

**Seed phrase.** A list of words that restores a cryptocurrency wallet. Anyone who has it controls the funds, so keep it offline. See [chapter 11](11-files-cloud-backups.md).

**Sideloading.** Installing apps from outside the official app store. It raises the risk of malware. See [chapter 09](09-phones.md).

**SIM (subscriber identity module).** The card or eSIM that connects your phone to a mobile carrier and ties it to your number. See [chapter 09](09-phones.md).

**SIM swapping.** Convincing a carrier to move your number to an attacker's SIM, to intercept SMS codes and calls.

**SMS.** Plain text messages carried by the mobile network. Not encrypted end to end and readable by carriers. See [chapter 07](07-messaging.md).

**Social engineering.** Manipulating people, rather than breaking technology, to obtain access or information. Phishing is the best-known form. See [chapter 03](03-accounts.md).

**Spyware / stalkerware.** Software that secretly monitors a device's activity, messages, or location. See [chapter 23](23-if-compromised.md).

**SSD (solid-state drive).** Flash storage. Overwriting is not a reliable way to erase it, so encrypt from the start. See [chapter 11](11-files-cloud-backups.md).

**SSH (Secure Shell).** A protocol for remotely logging in to a computer. Turn it off if unused. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## T

**Tails.** A privacy-focused operating system that runs from a USB stick and leaves no trace on the computer. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Tamper-evident seal.** A sticker or marking that shows if a device or port has been opened or altered. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Telemetry.** Diagnostic and usage data that software and devices send back to their makers. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Threat level.** A rating, from 0 to 6, of how determined an adversary you face, used to decide how much protection is worthwhile. See [chapter 02](02-threat-models.md).

**Threat model.** A written assessment of what you protect, from whom, and how much effort the protection is worth. See [chapter 02](02-threat-models.md).

**TLS (Transport Layer Security).** The encryption that protects data in transit, including HTTPS connections. See [chapter 06](06-search-dns-network.md).

**Tor.** A network that relays traffic through several nodes to separate your identity from your destination. See [chapter 21](21-anonymity-tools.md).

**TPM (Trusted Platform Module).** A chip that stores encryption keys and releases them only if the boot process is unchanged. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**Tracker.** A script, pixel, or SDK that records activity across sites and apps.

**Tracking pixel.** A tiny, usually invisible image in an email or web page that reports when it is opened or viewed. See [chapter 04](04-email.md).

**Traffic correlation.** Matching the timing and volume of traffic at both ends of a connection to link a user to a destination.

**Transparency report.** A provider's published statistics on government and legal requests for user data. See [chapter 20](20-metadata.md).

**Two-factor authentication (2FA).** Signing in with a second proof in addition to a password, such as a key, app code, or passkey.

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## U

**UPnP (Universal Plug and Play).** A router feature that lets devices open ports automatically. Turn it off if you do not need it. See [chapter 13](13-home-and-devices.md).

**USB data blocker.** An adapter that allows charging but blocks data transfer. See [chapter 22](22-travel-and-public-space.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## V

**Virtual card.** A temporary or masked card number linked to your real account, which limits the damage from a merchant breach. See [chapter 12](12-payments.md).

**Virtual machine.** A computer simulated by software, used to isolate risky or sensitive activity. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

**VPN (virtual private network).** An encrypted tunnel to a provider's server that hides traffic from the local network and shows websites the provider's IP address. See [chapter 06](06-search-dns-network.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## W

**WHOIS.** A public record of who registered a domain name. Registrars offer privacy services to hide your details. See [chapter 15](15-data-brokers-and-breaches.md).

**WPA2 / WPA3.** Wi-Fi security standards. Use WPA3 where devices support it. See [chapter 06](06-search-dns-network.md).

**WPS (Wi-Fi Protected Setup).** A convenience feature for joining a Wi-Fi network that weakens security. Turn it off. See [chapter 06](06-search-dns-network.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## X

**XProtect, Gatekeeper, and SmartScreen.** Built-in malware and download protections on macOS (XProtect, Gatekeeper) and Windows (SmartScreen). Leave them on. See [chapter 10](10-computers.md).

[↑ Back to TOC](#toc)

## Z

**Zero-knowledge (storage).** A design where the provider stores your data but cannot decrypt it. See [chapter 11](11-files-cloud-backups.md).
