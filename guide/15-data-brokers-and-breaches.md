# 15. Data brokers and breaches

Previous: [Households](14-households.md) | [Home](../README.md) | Next: [Account takeover](16-account-takeover.md)

## Who this is for

Everyone. Your information is already in places you did not choose. This chapter covers how to reduce it, limit the harm, and respond when a company leaks it.

## What leaks

- **Data brokers.** Companies that collect names, addresses, phone numbers, relatives, employment, purchases, and inferred interests, and sell them. In some countries, "people search" sites publish them.
- **Public records.** Property records, court records, business registrations, and voter files.
- **Breaches.** A company you gave data to loses it. Leaked data circulates for years and feeds phishing and identity theft.
- **Your own sharing.** Surveys, sweepstakes, warranty cards, and apps that sell your data.

## Steps

### 1. Find out what has leaked

- Check your email addresses and phone numbers against a breach notification service such as Have I Been Pwned, and subscribe to alerts.
- Note which accounts appear, and what was exposed: passwords, payment details, government identifiers, health data.

### 2. Respond to a breach

Act in this order:

1. **Passwords.** Change the password on the breached account, and on any other account where you used it. A password manager makes this quick ([chapter 03](03-accounts.md)).
2. **2FA.** Turn it on, and sign out of other sessions.
3. **Payment details.** If card details were exposed, replace the card ([chapter 12](12-payments.md)).
4. **Identity details.** If government ID numbers, dates of birth, or similar were exposed, consider a credit freeze or fraud alert with the credit bureaus in your country. A freeze is free in many countries and blocks new credit in your name until you lift it.
5. **Expect phishing.** Attackers use leaked details to write convincing messages. Treat unexpected contact referencing the breach with suspicion, and reach the company through its official site.
6. **Record.** Keep a note of the date, what was exposed, and what you did.

### 3. Reduce what you give out

- Share the minimum that a form requires. Use aliases and a secondary phone number ([chapter 04](04-email.md)).
- Do not give your real date of birth, phone number, or address where it is optional and not verified.
- Decline marketing opt-ins, and decline "sell or share my personal information" where the law lets you.
- Ask whether a company needs to store your data, and ask it to delete data you no longer want held.

### 4. Opt out of data brokers

- Look for a list of major brokers in your country and region, and follow each site's opt-out process. Expect to submit your name, address, and a verification email.
- Search for your name, phone number, and address and note what appears. Repeat every few months, because brokers re-add records.
- Use privacy laws where they apply: access, deletion, and opt-out rights exist in the EU and UK (GDPR), California (CCPA/CPRA), and other places. A request for access shows what a company holds.
- Paid "delete me" services can save time. They cover a limited list of brokers, rarely reach all of them, and must be re-run regularly. Compare the list of brokers they cover, check the company's reputation, and note that they need your personal details to work.

### 5. Reduce public exposure

- Check whether your address appears in public records and whether your jurisdiction has a way to suppress it for safety reasons.
- Use a business address or registered agent for business registrations where allowed.
- Review domain registrations and make sure WHOIS privacy is on.
- Search for your name and photo, and ask sites to remove content that harms you.

### 6. Delete old accounts and data

- List services you have used in the last decade. Delete accounts you no longer use, and request deletion of data.
- Use "delete my data" or "right to be forgotten" requests where available.

## What this does not fix

- Data already sold or leaked cannot be recalled. Opt-outs reduce future exposure.
- Brokers add records again from new sources, so this is routine maintenance, not a one-time task.
- Laws differ. Opt-out rights may be unavailable where you live.

## Checklist

- [ ] My email addresses are subscribed to a breach alert.
- [ ] I have a breach response routine (password, 2FA, card, freeze, phishing watch).
- [ ] I limit optional information on forms.
- [ ] I opted out of the main people-search sites in my country, and set a reminder to recheck.
- [ ] I deleted old accounts I no longer use.
