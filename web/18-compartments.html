18. Compartments

On this page
  • Who this is for
  • What links identities
  • Steps
  • What this does not fix
  • Checklist

Who this is for

People who need two or more identities kept apart: for example a public name and a private life, a whistleblowing or reporting role, an activist account, a support-group persona, or a safe account used when escaping an abusive situation.

Compartmentalization means keeping identities, devices, and accounts separate so that a problem in one does not expose the others. It reduces risk. It does not make an identity anonymous.

What links identities

Identities usually connect through ordinary details, not clever attacks:

Link Example
Recovery details The same recovery email or phone number on two accounts
Payment One card, name on a receipt, shipping address
Username and profile Reused handle, photo, bio
Network Same home IP address or the same Wi-Fi
Device Same phone, browser profile, or app install
Contacts The same friends followed or messaged
Behavior Same active hours, writing style, vocabulary, interests
Photos Metadata, backgrounds, faces, reflections
Time Posting right after a real-life event
Cloud accounts One cloud account backing up both identities' devices

One slip can join the identities, and the join is usually permanent.

Steps

1. Define each compartment

For each identity, write down:

  • Purpose and who knows about it.
  • Which accounts, email, phone number, devices, and payment methods belong to it.
  • What it must never touch (names, places, people, networks from another identity).

2. Separate the building blocks

  • Email: a dedicated address per identity, created without using another identity's email or number for recovery.
  • Phone number: a separate number if the identity needs one. Know that many providers require ID in your country, which links the number to your legal name.
  • Passwords: a separate password manager vault, or a separate manager, per identity. Use a different master passphrase.
  • Payments: keep identities with different payment methods when payment records matter. Be aware that the legal name on a card links the identity to you.
  • Devices: ideally a separate device per high-risk identity. A new device, bought without linking it to your main accounts, is stronger than a profile on a shared one. Do not sign in to your regular cloud or app-store accounts on it.
  • Browsers: at minimum a separate browser profile; better, a separate browser.
  • Network: avoid connecting a sensitive identity from the same home or work network as your real identity if the adversary can see network records. See chapter 21 for tools and their limits.

3. Keep habits apart

  • Use different usernames, avatars, writing style, and interests.
  • Do not post at times that match your real-life routine.
  • Do not mention your real life, location, workplace, or family in the separate identity.
  • Do not reuse photos or images across identities. Strip metadata from images (chapter 08).
  • Do not message across identities, such as using one account to ask another for help.

4. Create the identity cleanly

  • Start from clean devices and clean networks. If you create the account while signed in to your normal accounts, the platform may link them.
  • Do not import contacts from your main phone.
  • Avoid "Sign in with" connections.

5. Limit the damage of a slip

  • Decide in advance what you will do if the link is exposed: shut down the account, notify people, and change related passwords.
  • Keep a record, offline, of what each identity has used so that you can check for cross-links.
  • Review each compartment periodically for leaks: new contacts, old photos, tags by other people.

6. Handle other people

  • Tell the few people who need to know only what they need to know.
  • Ask them not to tag, mention, or forward across identities.
  • Remember that their phone books and backups hold your details.

What this does not fix

  • Platforms may link accounts through signals you did not think of, such as device fingerprints, contact uploads, or behavioral patterns.
  • A court order or provider breach can reveal the underlying details.
  • Compartments take discipline. One tired mistake joins them.
