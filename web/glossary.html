Glossary

0-9 A B C D E F G H I K L M O P Q R S T U V W X Z

See also the Disclaimer and Limitation of Liability.

0-9

3-2-1 backup rule. Keep three copies of your data, on two kinds of storage, with one off-site. See chapter 11.

↑ A–Z

A

Account takeover. An attacker gaining control of one of your accounts, usually through a reused password, phishing, or a SIM swap. See chapter 16.

ACR (automatic content recognition). A smart-TV feature that identifies what is on screen and reports viewing data to the manufacturer or advertisers. See chapter 13.

Advertising ID. A device-level identifier that lets ad networks follow you across apps. See chapter 09.

AES / XTS-AES. AES is a widely used encryption standard. XTS-AES is the mode commonly used for disk encryption. See chapter 10.

Alias. A secondary email address that forwards to your main inbox, used to avoid sharing your real address. See chapter 04.

Anonymity. The inability of others to link an action to your real identity. Distinct from privacy and security. See chapter 01.

Attack surface. All the ways an attacker could try to get in: installed software, open services, ports, extensions, and accounts. Fewer means safer. See chapter 10.

Authenticator app. An app that generates time-based one-time codes for two-factor authentication. See chapter 03.

↑ A–Z

B

Biometrics. Fingerprint, face, or similar unlock methods. Convenient, but in some places easier for authorities to compel than a passcode. See chapter 09 and chapter 19.

BitLocker. Windows full-disk encryption. See chapter 10.

Bridge (Tor). An unlisted entry point to the Tor network, used where Tor is blocked or where being seen to use Tor is risky. See chapter 21.

↑ A–Z

C

Captive portal. The sign-in or terms page shown by public Wi-Fi before you can connect. Do not enter passwords on one you do not recognize. See chapter 06.

CCPA / CPRA. California's consumer-privacy laws, which give residents rights to access, delete, and opt out of the sale of personal data. See chapter 15.

Cold-boot attack. Recovering encryption keys from a computer's memory shortly after it is shut down or while it is asleep. Shutting down fully and waiting reduces the risk. See chapter 10.

Compartmentalization. Keeping identities, accounts, and devices separate so that a problem in one does not expose the others. See chapter 18.

Content blocker. A browser extension that blocks ads and many trackers, such as uBlock Origin. See chapter 05.

Cookie (third-party). A small file a website stores in your browser. Third-party cookies come from other companies embedded in the page and let them recognize you across sites. See chapter 05.

Credential stuffing. An attack that tries leaked email and password pairs on other sites, relying on reuse.

Credit freeze. A lock on your credit file that blocks new credit in your name until you lift it. See chapter 15.

Cryptographic erase. Making encrypted data unreadable by destroying the encryption key instead of overwriting the data. Fast and reliable for retiring encrypted drives. See chapter 10.

↑ A–Z

D

Data breach. An incident in which a company loses or exposes data it held about you. See chapter 15.

Data broker. A company that collects, combines, and sells personal data. See chapter 15.

Disappearing messages. A messaging feature that deletes messages from the devices after a set time. It cannot stop a recipient from copying or screenshotting. See chapter 07.

DMA (direct memory access) attack. Using a device plugged into a port such as Thunderbolt to read a computer's memory directly. DMA protection and IOMMU settings reduce the risk. See chapter 10.

DNS (Domain Name System). The system that turns site names into addresses. A DNS resolver sees which sites you look up. See chapter 06.

DNS over HTTPS / TLS. Encrypted DNS lookups that hide queries from the local network.

Doxxing. Publishing someone's private information, such as their home address or workplace, to expose or harass them. See chapter 08 and chapter 17.

↑ A–Z

E

EFI system partition. A small partition that holds boot software. It is usually not encrypted, which is why boot protections matter. See chapter 10.

End-to-end encryption (E2EE). Encryption where only the sender and recipient devices hold the keys, so the service provider cannot read the content. See chapter 07.

Evil maid attack. Tampering with a computer's boot process or hardware by someone who gets brief physical access, to capture your passphrase later. See chapter 10.

Exit relay. The last Tor node, which connects to the destination and can see unencrypted traffic.

↑ A–Z

F

FIDO2 / WebAuthn. Standards behind hardware security keys and passkeys that resist phishing.

FileVault. macOS full-disk encryption. See chapter 10.

Fingerprinting. Identifying a browser or device from its combination of settings and features, without cookies. See chapter 05.

Firmware (UEFI/BIOS). The low-level software that starts a computer before the operating system loads. A firmware password and Secure Boot protect it.

Full-disk encryption. Encryption of the whole storage device, protecting data when the device is off. See chapter 10.

↑ A–Z

G

GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation). The European Union's data-protection law, which gives people rights to access, correct, delete, and object to the use of their data. See chapter 15.

GPS (Global Positioning System). Satellite-based positioning used by phones and other devices to find precise location. See chapter 09.

Guard relay. The first relay a Tor connection passes through. It sees your real IP address but not your destination. See chapter 21.

↑ A–Z

H

Hardening. Reducing a system's attack surface and tightening its settings so that it is harder to compromise. See chapter 10.

Hardware security key. A small physical device used for sign-in that resists phishing, using FIDO2/WebAuthn. See chapter 03.

HTTPS. Encrypted web connections, which protect page content in transit.

↑ A–Z

I

IOMMU. A hardware feature that limits which memory a connected device can access, used for DMA protection. See chapter 10.

IoT (Internet of Things). Everyday devices connected to the internet, such as cameras, speakers, TVs, and appliances. See chapter 13.

IP address. The network address that identifies your connection to the sites and services you use. It reveals approximate location and your internet provider. See chapter 06.

↑ A–Z

K

Keylogger. Software or hardware that records what you type, including passwords and passphrases. See chapter 10.

↑ A–Z

L

Linkage. Connecting separate accounts, identities, or records to the same person, for example through a shared username, photo, or recovery address. See chapter 08 and chapter 18.

Lockdown feature. A phone setting or button combination that quickly disables biometrics and requires the passcode, or enables a high-security mode. See chapter 19.

LUKS. The standard Linux disk-encryption format. See chapter 10.

↑ A–Z

M

Malware. Malicious software, including spyware, keyloggers, ransomware, and remote-access tools. See chapter 10.

Measured boot. A startup process in which the TPM records the boot software and only releases the disk key if it is unchanged. See chapter 10.

Metadata. Data about communications and activity (who, when, where, how much), as opposed to the content. See chapter 20.

↑ A–Z

O

Onion service. A site reachable only through Tor (an address ending in .onion), protecting both the visitor and the site. See chapter 21.

Open source. Software whose code is public, so independent experts can review it. This helps, but does not guarantee that it is secure.

OpenPGP. A standard for encrypting and signing email and files. Powerful, but hard to use safely. See chapter 04.

↑ A–Z

P

Passkey. A cryptographic sign-in credential, stored on a device or in a password manager, that replaces a password and resists phishing. See chapter 03.

Passphrase. A long password made of several random words or a long sentence. See chapter 03.

Password manager. An app that generates, stores, and fills in unique passwords for every account. See chapter 03.

People-search site. A website that publishes names, addresses, phone numbers, and relatives gathered from public records and data brokers. See chapter 15.

Phishing. Deceptive messages or sites designed to steal credentials or money.

Port-out protection. A carrier setting that blocks moving your number to another provider without extra verification, used against SIM swapping.

Privacy. Control over who can see or collect information about you, and how it is used.

Private browsing. A browser mode that does not save history or cookies locally after the window closes. It does not hide your activity from websites, your network, or your internet provider. See chapter 05.

Pseudonym. A name other than your legal name used for an account or identity. It reduces linkage but does not provide anonymity. See chapter 08.

↑ A–Z

Q

Qubes OS. A security-focused operating system that isolates activities in separate virtual machines. See chapter 10.

↑ A–Z

R

Ransomware. Malware that encrypts your files and demands payment to unlock them. Offline backups are the best defense. See chapter 10.

RCS (Rich Communication Services). The newer standard that extends SMS with features such as read receipts and group chat. Not end-to-end encrypted in every case. See chapter 07.

RDP (Remote Desktop Protocol). Windows remote-control feature. Leave it off unless you need it. See chapter 10.

Recovery key. A secret code that restores access to an encrypted account or disk. Losing it can mean permanent loss of data.

↑ A–Z

S

Safety number. A code that two people compare to confirm they are talking to each other and not to an impersonator. See chapter 07.

Sandbox. An isolated environment that limits what a program can access, used by browsers, phones, and some desktop apps. See chapter 10.

Sealed sender. A messaging feature that hides the sender's identity from the service when delivering a message. See chapter 20.

Secure Boot. A firmware feature that only starts operating-system software signed by trusted keys, making boot-time tampering harder. See chapter 10.

Security. Protection against unauthorized access to your accounts, devices, and data.

Seed phrase. A list of words that restores a cryptocurrency wallet. Anyone who has it controls the funds, so keep it offline. See chapter 11.

Sideloading. Installing apps from outside the official app store. It raises the risk of malware. See chapter 09.

SIM (subscriber identity module). The card or eSIM that connects your phone to a mobile carrier and ties it to your number. See chapter 09.

SIM swapping. Convincing a carrier to move your number to an attacker's SIM, to intercept SMS codes and calls.

SMS. Plain text messages carried by the mobile network. Not encrypted end to end and readable by carriers. See chapter 07.

Social engineering. Manipulating people, rather than breaking technology, to obtain access or information. Phishing is the best-known form. See chapter 03.

Spyware / stalkerware. Software that secretly monitors a device's activity, messages, or location. See chapter 23.

SSD (solid-state drive). Flash storage. Overwriting is not a reliable way to erase it, so encrypt from the start. See chapter 11.

SSH (Secure Shell). A protocol for remotely logging in to a computer. Turn it off if unused. See chapter 10.

↑ A–Z

T

Tails. A privacy-focused operating system that runs from a USB stick and leaves no trace on the computer. See chapter 10.

Tamper-evident seal. A sticker or marking that shows if a device or port has been opened or altered. See chapter 10.

Telemetry. Diagnostic and usage data that software and devices send back to their makers. See chapter 10.

Threat level. A rating, from 0 to 6, of how determined an adversary you face, used to decide how much protection is worthwhile. See chapter 02.

Threat model. A written assessment of what you protect, from whom, and how much effort the protection is worth. See chapter 02.

TLS (Transport Layer Security). The encryption that protects data in transit, including HTTPS connections. See chapter 06.

Tor. A network that relays traffic through several nodes to separate your identity from your destination. See chapter 21.

TPM (Trusted Platform Module). A chip that stores encryption keys and releases them only if the boot process is unchanged. See chapter 10.

Tracker. A script, pixel, or SDK that records activity across sites and apps.

Tracking pixel. A tiny, usually invisible image in an email or web page that reports when it is opened or viewed. See chapter 04.

Traffic correlation. Matching the timing and volume of traffic at both ends of a connection to link a user to a destination.

Transparency report. A provider's published statistics on government and legal requests for user data. See chapter 20.

Two-factor authentication (2FA). Signing in with a second proof in addition to a password, such as a key, app code, or passkey.

↑ A–Z

U

UPnP (Universal Plug and Play). A router feature that lets devices open ports automatically. Turn it off if you do not need it. See chapter 13.

USB data blocker. An adapter that allows charging but blocks data transfer. See chapter 22.

↑ A–Z

V

Virtual card. A temporary or masked card number linked to your real account, which limits the damage from a merchant breach. See chapter 12.

Virtual machine. A computer simulated by software, used to isolate risky or sensitive activity. See chapter 10.

VPN (virtual private network). An encrypted tunnel to a provider's server that hides traffic from the local network and shows websites the provider's IP address. See chapter 06.

↑ A–Z

W

WHOIS. A public record of who registered a domain name. Registrars offer privacy services to hide your details. See chapter 15.

WPA2 / WPA3. Wi-Fi security standards. Use WPA3 where devices support it. See chapter 06.

WPS (Wi-Fi Protected Setup). A convenience feature for joining a Wi-Fi network that weakens security. Turn it off. See chapter 06.

↑ A–Z

X

XProtect, Gatekeeper, and SmartScreen. Built-in malware and download protections on macOS (XProtect, Gatekeeper) and Windows (SmartScreen). Leave them on. See chapter 10.

↑ A–Z

Z

Zero-knowledge (storage). A design where the provider stores your data but cannot decrypt it. See chapter 11.
