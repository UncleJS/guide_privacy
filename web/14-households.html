14. Households

On this page
  • Who this is for
  • What leaks
  • Steps
  • What this does not fix

Who this is for

People who share homes, devices, accounts, or plans with partners, family, children, or roommates. This chapter is about protecting your own privacy and respecting other people's, including the privacy of children as they grow. It does not describe how to monitor another adult.

What leaks

  • Shared devices and accounts. Browsing history, saved passwords, and message previews appear to everyone who uses them.
  • Family plans and location sharing. Family accounts often show locations, purchases, and app use to the organizer.
  • Shared cloud storage and photo libraries. Everything uploaded may be visible to everyone in the group.
  • Billing and statements. Bills reveal services, medical visits, and subscriptions.
  • Children's data. Photos, school information, and app use that a child did not choose to share.
  • Former partners. Shared accounts and old devices can keep access after a relationship ends.

Steps

1. Give each person their own account and device where possible

  • Use separate user accounts on shared computers and tablets.
  • Avoid sharing a single email or cloud account across the household.
  • Set screen locks on every device.

2. Share deliberately

  • Share specific items, not entire accounts. Shared folders, family calendars, and group password-manager vaults let you share what is needed without sharing everything.
  • Agree on what is shared, and write it down. Revisit as circumstances change.
  • Use a password manager's sharing feature for shared logins, rather than sending passwords in messages.

3. Location sharing

  • Use location sharing with consent from the person being located. Make it visible so that everyone knows who can see what, and make it easy to switch off.
  • Time-limit sharing for trips or events.
  • Review who can see your location in your phone and apps regularly, and remove old shares.

4. Children and teenagers

  • Explain what privacy is in terms they understand, and show them how to use settings. Privacy habits formed early last.
  • Choose age-appropriate tools, set accounts to private, and review settings together.
  • Think before posting photos of children. Do not share details that reveal a child's school, routine, or full name, and consider whether the child would want the image online later.
  • As children grow, reduce monitoring and increase their control. Tell them what is monitored and why.
  • Teach them to recognize scams, grooming, and pressure, and that they can come to you without fear of punishment.
  • Review the privacy practices of educational apps and school systems, and ask the school how it protects pupil data.

5. Relationships and shared access

  • Treat access to another adult's accounts, devices, or location without their knowledge as a violation of their privacy that may be illegal.
  • When a relationship changes, change shared passwords, remove their access from shared accounts, plans, and devices, and review location sharing, shared calendars, and smart-home accounts. Do this for your own accounts.
  • If you suspect that someone is monitoring you, see chapter 17 and chapter 23. If you are in danger, use a device the other person has never had access to, and contact a support organization before changing things that might alert them.

6. Older relatives

  • Help them with password managers, updates, and scam awareness.
  • Agree on what you will and will not have access to, and keep the arrangement documented.
  • Set up trusted-contact and legacy features, so that someone can help if they are incapacitated.

7. Your own legacy

  • Use the legacy-contact or inactive-account features of your key services, or record in your password manager how to reach important accounts.

What this does not fix

  • Group privacy is only as strong as the least careful member.
  • Sharing arrangements are social agreements. Software settings support them but do not replace conversation.
